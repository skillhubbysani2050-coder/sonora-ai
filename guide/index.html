Your browser already has voices.
Here is where they stop.

Sonora plays speech with voices that are already installed on your device. That keeps the prototype simple and cheap to run, but it also means the quality, the language and even the privacy of a sentence depend on your machine and browser, not on Sonora. This page explains what actually happens, which voices are not local, and what a hosted speech API would add.

What happens when you press play

Three things, in this order:

  1. Your text goes into the browser's built-in speech engine, the Web Speech API. On the page that is the speechSynthesis object.
  2. The browser asks the operating system for a voice. Windows, macOS, iOS, Android and most Linux desktops each ship their own set, so the list differs from machine to machine.
  3. That voice renders the sentence and the browser plays it through your speakers.

Sonora has no server in this path. It never receives your text, and nothing is stored, because there is nowhere to store it yet.

One exception matters. Not every voice in that list is local. Some are synthesised online by the browser maker and only played back in the page.

Which voices are local, and which are not

Names differ by platform and change between versions, but the pattern is fairly consistent:

Usually runs on your device

  • Microsoft voices on Windows (David, Zira, Mark and the newer natural voices once downloaded)
  • Apple voices on macOS and iOS (Samantha, Daniel, Karen, and any "Enhanced" or "Premium" voice you installed)
  • eSpeak or speech-dispatcher voices on Linux

Usually synthesised online

  • Voices whose name starts with "Google" in Chrome — the text is sent to Google to be turned into speech
  • The online "Natural" voices in Microsoft Edge
  • Some cloud voices on Android, depending on the speech engine the phone ships with

Labels are not a guarantee, so test it directly: turn off Wi-Fi and mobile data, then press play in the studio. A local voice keeps speaking. An online voice fails or falls back to a robotic one.

What the browser cannot do

  • No downloadable audio. The browser plays speech; it does not hand you an MP3. There is no supported way to capture the output of speechSynthesis.
  • No SSML. You get rate, pitch, volume and language. You cannot ask for a pause, an emphasis or a pronunciation the way a hosted API lets you.
  • No word timings. Nothing tells you when each word is spoken, so captions and lip-sync are guesswork.
  • The voice list is the visitor's, not yours. A voice that sounds good on your laptop may not exist on a visitor's phone, so the same page sounds different to every person.
  • Mobile browsers need a tap first. On iOS, speech cannot start without a user gesture, so nothing can play on page load.
  • Long text can be cut off. Some browsers stop after a while or drop the queue; splitting long passages into sentences is the usual workaround.

That trade is the whole decision. A hosted API buys consistency and control, and pays for it in money, latency and a new place where text exists. For a prototype with no accounts and no billing, the browser's own voices are the honest default: they work today, they cost nothing, and nobody has to trust a backend that does not exist yet.

Offering hosted voices later would mean more than adding a key to a page: a provider agreement, a server that holds the key so it never reaches the browser, usage metering so one visitor cannot run up the bill, per-voice licensing, and limits against abuse.

Where Sonora stands

Right now the studio plays text with your device's voices, the assistant in the agent card is scripted rather than a live model, and the Python snippet is illustrative — there is no hosted Sonora API to call. The FAQ says the same thing in shorter form, and the What's new log records each change to the page.

Check your own device in a minute

  1. Open the studio and look at the voice menu.
  2. Note which names start with "Google" or say "Online" or "Natural".
  3. Turn off Wi-Fi and mobile data, then press play.
  4. If the voice still speaks, it lives on your device. If it stops, it was coming from the network.
